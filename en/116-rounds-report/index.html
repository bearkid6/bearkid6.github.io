The one-page conclusion: the final choice

The final choice after 116 rounds of research is the simplest setup: in US dollars, hold an all-world stock ETF forever; in renminbi, buy a few low-fee CSI 1000 (中证 1000) enhanced index funds; invest new money as soon as you have it; no market timing. The three most important findings:

  1. Only one source of excess return can be reliably captured: CSI 1000 enhanced index funds (about 3–5 percentage points a year). The other 100-plus approaches either earn less after costs or stopped working once they became public and popular.
  2. Tax changes the answer: the moving-average timing rule reduces drawdowns in 10 markets, but for a Chinese resident who holds forever and files taxes as required, the 20% tax due on sales wipes out its return advantage, so buy and hold is the choice.
  3. Betting on a single country (including the US) carries real risk: across 16 developed countries over 150 years, the probability of losing money after inflation over a 30-year holding period is one in eight; a globally diversified portfolio never lost, and it did not earn less.

Abstract

This paper uses free public data to run 116 rounds of tests of whether an individual investor with very little capital who is a Chinese tax resident (中国税收居民) can reliably earn excess returns (September 28 to October 12, 2026; the data for round 113 could not be obtained). In every round, the hypothesis, the pass/fail criteria and a prior probability were registered in the research log before the data were examined; all results are net of trading costs, and those involving overseas assets are further calculated under Chinese individual income tax. There are five main findings.

First, US market timing: a “three-moving-average voting” rule chosen on 1927–1989 data returned 11.77% annualized out of sample in 1990–2026, versus 11.01% for the S&P 500, with maximum drawdowns of −37% vs −55%; applied unchanged to 4 developed-market regions outside the US and to 4 emerging markets, the rule held up. But its return advantage ranges from 0 to +1.1 percentage points depending on the rebalancing day, it has lagged by about 0.8 a year since 2009, and it is not significant after accounting for selection luck (Deflated Sharpe 0.01). It is “insurance against long bear markets,” not a reliable source of excess return.

Second, stock selection and anomalies: most of the 212 published US anomalies decayed after publication and are concentrated in small caps; the predictive power of A-share price-volume signals and of a self-built machine-learning model (monthly rank IC 0.11) is concentrated on the loser side, and long-only versions do not earn significant excess returns. Among company-announcement signals, buying after a large positive earnings pre-announcement (业绩预告) yields an excess return of +1.50% over 60 trading days (t = 2.82), but this has been negative in 2022–2025.

Third, Chinese mutual-fund (公募) enhanced index funds: in 2023–2025, CSI 1000 enhanced funds beat index ETFs by 4–8 percentage points a year after fees. After removing IPO income using about 100,000 offline IPO allocation (网下打新) records, the excess returns of CSI 300 and CSI 500 enhanced funds largely disappear, while CSI 1000 enhanced funds still have about 3.2% (t = 2.7) after controlling for size, turnover and low beta.

Fourth, tax and investment horizon change the optimal choice: when holding forever and filing as required, the timing rule’s after-tax return is the same as buy and hold (+0.03 percentage points a year); data for 16 developed countries in 1870–2020 show that betting on a single country and holding for 30 years has a 12.4% probability of a real loss, versus 0 for a global portfolio.

Fifth, the credibility of the research itself: 67 prior-probability forecasts averaged 38%, while 31% actually occurred; the Brier score was 0.145 (baseline 0.215), with systematic over-optimism that “my own idea will pass.”

The conclusion: for a small Chinese individual investor, very little excess return can be reliably captured from public data; the most valuable findings are what not to do, and the allocation choices that follow from tax, horizon and diversification across countries.

Abstract (English). Using only free public data, I ran more than 110 pre-registered tests (28 Sep – 12 Oct 2026) of whether a small Chinese retail investor can earn reliable excess returns after costs and Chinese personal income tax. Each test recorded its hypothesis, pass/fail rule and a prior probability before the data were examined. A three-moving-average timing rule chosen on 1927–1989 US data cut drawdowns out of sample (−37% vs −55% for 1990–2026) and replicated in eight other markets, but its return edge is small, not significant after selection (Deflated Sharpe 0.01), and disappears after tax for a buy-and-never-sell investor. Published anomalies, A-share price signals and machine-learning forecasts were concentrated on the short side. The one robust long-only source was CSI 1000 enhanced index funds: after removing IPO-allocation income, size and turnover factors and low beta, their alpha is about 3.2% a year (t = 2.7), while CSI 300/500 enhanced funds’ excess returns were largely IPO income. JST data for 16 countries (1870–2020) show a 12.4% chance of a negative real 30-year return for a single country versus none for a global portfolio. The result is a simpler plan than the one the project started with: a global accumulating ETF held forever, plus a few low-fee CSI 1000 enhanced funds.

1 Introduction

The question this study tries to answer is: can a Chinese individual investor who uses only public data and has very little capital (a few thousand US dollars at most) reliably earn more, after costs and taxes, than “what they would have done anyway” (buying an index, parking money in a money market fund)? Small capital means that minimum commissions, whole-share lots and subscription minimums all eat into thin edges; tax-resident status means that gains from overseas sales and dividends must be reported at 20%; the available channels are US brokers, A-shares and over-the-counter funds (场外基金), and cryptocurrency exchanges; convertible bonds, Southbound Stock Connect (港股通), exchange-traded options and the like have minimum-asset requirements, so a small account cannot open them.

The literature offers three pieces of background for this question. First, many published return anomalies decay after publication (McLean and Pontiff 2016) and are concentrated in small caps, where trading costs are high (Hou, Xue and Zhang 2020). Second, the more rules a backtest tries, the less credible the statistical significance of the best result (Harvey, Liu and Zhu 2016; Bailey and López de Prado 2014). Third, individual investors’ actual returns are often eaten up by trading, attention-driven buying and taxes (Barber and Odean 2000, 2008). This study treats these three points as methodological constraints rather than conclusions.

This paper makes three contributions.

  1. Within a single pre-registered framework, it compares US stocks, A-shares, mutual funds, cryptocurrencies, arbitrage and allocation questions on the same basis, and honestly reports all 116 rounds (most of which did not pass).
  2. It rebuilds, with free data, several analyses that usually require commercial databases: reconstructing the IPO income of mutual-fund enhanced index funds from offline allocation details, recovering liquidated funds, and measuring crowding among quantitative private funds (量化私募); and reconstructing point-in-time financial statements from SEC XBRL.
  3. It brings Chinese individual income tax, a perpetual holding horizon and US estate tax into the strategy comparison, showing that the same rule can lead to opposite conclusions for different investors.

Section 2 describes the research design and data; Sections 3–7 report results by topic (US market timing, stock selection and anomalies, enhanced index funds, other assets, investor-level questions); Section 8 discusses credibility and limitations; Section 9 concludes. The appendix lists all 116 rounds.

2 Research design and data

All 116 rounds followed the same process, with each step recorded in the research log in time order; the pass/fail criteria were written down before the results were seen and were not changed after the results came out.

The research process for each round · 5 steps, with a marginal-payoff check every 3 rounds

2.1 Pre-registration

Each round’s registration includes: why it is being done; what has already been seen (a contamination statement); sample, definitions and costs; pass/fail criteria; items that are only described and do not count toward the verdict; and the expected result and prior probability. A data check is done before the results are viewed, and changes to definitions can be made only at this step. Checks thought of only after seeing the results are labeled separately as “post hoc” and serve only as a reference; for example, after round 98 found that the earnings pre-announcement table excluded delisted companies, it recalculated using “stocks that survived to today” as the benchmark, but did not change the original verdict.

2.2 Out of sample and multiple testing

  • Splits: the US market-timing rule was chosen on 1927–1989 and tested on 1990–2026; the A-share machine-learning model was explored on 2006–2016 and, once finalized, tested only once on the hold-out period from 2016-10 onward.
  • Corrections: multiple tests within one round use the Holm correction; rules picked from many candidates use the Deflated Sharpe Ratio; stock-selection rules use a permutation test (randomly picking stocks from the same candidate pool, repeated one hundred times).
  • Discipline: directions already rejected are not retried with different parameters; a marginal-payoff check is written every 3 rounds, and if the gain is too small, the research switches to a different broad direction.

2.3 Costs and taxes

All results are net of trading costs. A-share event studies assume a round-trip cost of 0.25% (commission of 0.05% (万五), stamp duty on sales, market impact), and note the effect of the 5-yuan minimum per trade on small accounts; funds use after-fee net asset values. Overseas assets follow the Chinese individual income tax treatment: 20% on dividends and on gains from sales (the 10% US withholding under the China–US tax treaty is creditable), with gains and losses offset within the same tax year and no carryforward across years; the main specification assumes filing as required.

3 US market timing: the moving-average voting rule

Only one US market-timing rule survived, and its reliable value is reducing drawdowns, not earning more: out of sample in 1990–2026 it returned 11.77% annualized vs 11.01% for the S&P 500, with maximum drawdowns of −37% vs −55%.

3.1 The rule and the out-of-sample test

The rule (called v3) looks only at how many of the 150-, 200- and 250-day moving averages the S&P 500’s closing price is above: 0, 1, 2 or 3 correspond to 0.5, 0.75, 1 or 1.25 times equity exposure, implemented with 1x and 2x ETFs plus Treasuries; it is checked once at each month-end, and trades only when the deviation from target exceeds a tolerance. The rule was selected from about 330 candidate combinations on Ken French daily data for 1927–1989; data after 1990 had not been looked at before it was chosen.

The best in-sample candidates were of the “volatility targeting” type (adjusting exposure to recent volatility), and they all failed out of sample; pure trend (selling everything below the 200-day line) earned less than holding out of sample. Neither type was retried afterward.

3.3 Robustness

  • Rebalancing-date luck (round 15): moving the month-end check to any of the other 20 trading days in the month, the out-of-sample return advantage ranges from 0 to +1.1 percentage points, with a median of about +0.5.
  • Selection luck (round 20): the Deflated Sharpe Ratio computed over 330 candidates is 0.01, and the out-of-sample Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio is 0.75—the excess return is not significant.
  • Attribution: in CAPM, three-factor and Treynor–Mazuy regressions, α is significant in the full sample but not out of sample; timing ability shows up only at the annual scale.
  • Defensive position in intermediate-term Treasuries (rounds 18, 19; called v3.1): better than short-term bonds in all three periods; out-of-sample annualized return 12.51% vs 11.77% for the short-term-bond version (S&P 500 11.01%); compared with a fixed stock-bond portfolio of equal volatility, 12.5% / −32% vs 10.8% / −52%. The cost is larger losses when stocks and bonds fall together, as in the 1970s and 2022.
  • Renminbi basis (round 17): the drawdown advantage remains, at about 17 percentage points; the exchange rate is normally a cushion (monthly correlation −0.25), but in 2008 it amplified losses.

4 Stock selection and anomalies

None of the public stock-selection signals lets a small long-only investor reliably make money after costs: anomalies among US large caps have essentially disappeared after publication, the predictive power of A-share signals is concentrated on the “stocks to avoid” side, and earnings pre-announcement drift, the only signal that worked on the long side, has also vanished since 2022.

4.1 Multi-factor stock screener

The screener scores US stocks on momentum, valuation and growth, and holds a single stock at full position each time. Its backtested annualized return is about 17%, slightly above the S&P 500, but its maximum drawdown is −55% vs −34%; in the permutation test it beat only 86% of random stock picks (threshold 95%), and its CAPM α is not significant. Its fundamental components add nothing either: the “both revenue and net profit growing” filter has t = −0.42 in S&P 500 point-in-time data (round 30), and the valuation score’s rank IC is 0.003 (round 31).

4.2 Published US anomalies

  • 18 price-based factors in the S&P 500 are all insignificant after Holm correction (round 1); in French’s all-market data, price-based anomalies among large caps have essentially disappeared since 2000 (round 2).
  • Of the 212 anomalies published by Chen–Zimmermann, 28% are still significant after publication under the original papers’ methods, which include micro-caps; in the large-cap half only 4% remain, and after multiple-testing correction not one is left (round 3)—anomalies live mainly in small caps, where trading costs are high.
  • The only one that holds up across data sources is profitability: using SEC XBRL point-in-time statements it passed pre-registration in the S&P 500 (round 4), but the long-only version has t = 1.64 (round 6), and outside the US t = 0.42 (round 10).
  • Insider buying has t = 0.41 (round 8); self-mined price-volume factors in the S&P 500 already failed in the exploration period (round 16); industry momentum has t = 1.76 after publication, which under the rules is a fail, with no parameter tweaking to make it pass (round 40).

4.3 A-share price-volume signals and machine learning

  • Abnormal turnover and short-term reversal effects are strong (t > 7), but the premium is concentrated on the short side (round 11).
  • 53 daily features + ridge regression: explored on 2006–2016 and, once finalized, tested only once on the hold-out period from 2016-10 onward. The monthly rank IC is 0.108 (t = 10.3), but going long only the top 10% earns +4.3% a year after costs, with t = 1.72, below the threshold of 2, so it fails (round 13). The same model’s “most likely to fall” list underperformed the whole market in 78% of months during the hold-out period, by −1.90% a month on average (t = −7.4); it has been switched to monthly forward tracking (round 94).
  • The short-term trades common among retail investors all lose money: holding recent IPOs (次新股) for 3 years after their shares first stop hitting the daily limit (开板) returns −9.6% a year relative to stocks of the same market cap (t = −3.3, round 93); buying at the open the day after a limit-up returns −0.91% per trade (t = −27, round 95); bottom-fishing at the open the day after a limit-down returns −1.39% per trade (t = −21, round 97). The losses are concentrated overnight: the T+1 rule (shares bought today cannot be sold until the next trading day) keeps retail investors from capturing the daytime rebound, consistent with the attention-driven buying in Barber and Odean (2008).

5 Chinese mutual-fund enhanced index funds

Mutual-fund enhanced index funds are one of the few channels in this study through which small investors can earn A-share excess returns, but only the excess of CSI 1000 enhanced funds remains significant after removing IPO income and style effects; the “excess” of CSI 300 (沪深 300) and CSI 500 (中证 500) enhanced funds came mainly from the IPO-subscription bonus that comes with mutual-fund status, and it has decayed since 2021.

5.1 Excess returns after fees

Comparing 207 CSI 300, CSI 500 and CSI 1000 enhanced funds with the corresponding index ETFs (round 21): CSI 1000 enhanced funds earned +3.9, +4.5 and +7.7 percentage points a year in 2023–2025, with 80–89% of funds outperforming. Annual excess returns within each category do not persist (autocorrelation −0.24 to +0.18), so picking funds by last year’s performance has no basis; buying 5 at random, even the worst 5% of outcomes beat the ETF by 1.4–3.3 percentage points a year (round 47). Lower-fee funds have higher excess returns (cross-sectional slope t = −3.1, round 57).

5.3 Decay and crowding

Mutual-fund AUM (round 44), the correlation of excess returns across funds (46), total market turnover (48), the cross-sectional dispersion of stock returns (56) and fund flows (60) all fail to explain the decay of CSI 500 enhanced funds. Private fund AUM is not public, but private products also take part in offline IPO subscriptions: counting entries on the allocation-account lists, the number of private 500-enhanced products participating in IPO subscriptions grew from 3 at the end of 2019 to 132 at the end of 2021 and about 190 in 2022, matching the timing of the collapse in excess returns, but the regression t = −1.62 is not significant (round 62).

This yields a prediction that can be tested going forward: private 1000-enhanced products grew from 83 to 173 over 2025Q3 → 2026Q3 (from 0.90% → 1.36% of all allocation accounts, already above the 0.86% for 500-enhanced products at the end of 2021), while the 1000-enhanced category’s excess return for January–September 2026 is still +3.1%. If the crowding explanation holds, 1000-enhanced excess returns should fall markedly over the next year or two.

5.4 Other tests

  • Index reconstitution (rounds 49–52): in the June and December reconstitution months, monthly excess returns are higher by 0.45 (500 enhanced, t = 2.24) and 0.68 (1000 enhanced, t = 2.42) percentage points; deleted stocks underperform by 2.5% before the change takes effect (t = −3.3), but this cannot be linked event by event to reconstitution-period excess, and full holdings show no underweighting either (round 65).
  • Survivorship bias (round 59): a mutual fund’s allocation-account code is its fund code, so liquidated funds can be recovered; 0 1000-enhanced funds were liquidated, and adding back the liquidated 500 / 300 funds changes the annual average by < 0.6 percentage points.
  • Exit rule (round 102): “switch back to the ETF if the category’s excess over the past 12 months is negative” loses money in all three categories (−1.58 / −0.60 / −0.44 a year)—500-enhanced excess went from high to low without turning negative, so the rule exits too late. Instead, monitor the number of private products and the category excess each year, and consider switching only when both conditions are met.
  • Exchange-traded vs over-the-counter (round 106): 6 exchange-traded enhanced ETFs have fees about 0.45 percentage points lower and average excess returns 1.0–1.5 higher in 2024–2025, with almost the same median; most are very small.
  • Small-cap stampede and sharp rally (round 110): from 2023-12-29 → 2024-02-05 the CSI 1000 fell 27.1%, while the 13 1000-enhanced funds already operating at the start of 2023 had average excess returns of +2.48% (12 positive); cross-sectionally this is unrelated to the “smaller-cap” exposure from 2023 daily regressions (t −0.55); one reason is that mutual funds’ beta is about 0.9. The cost comes in sharp rallies: in the two weeks of the 9·24 rally (September 24), the index rose 38.7%, while 1000-enhanced excess was −2.93% and 500-enhanced −4.28%, with every fund negative. The deepest drawdown of category cumulative excess was −6.2%, recovered in about 7 months.
  • Same company across products (round 112): within the same year, a company’s 500 / 300 enhanced and 1000 enhanced excess returns have rank correlations of 0.41–0.61; splitting into halves by the historical excess of a company’s other enhanced products, the top half’s 1000-enhanced funds are 1.9 percentage points higher the next year, but t = 1.87, which fails under the pre-registration.
  • Fund manager changes (round 111): among 397 manager changes in the 207 enhanced index funds, there were 27 complete replacements with a full 12 months before and after: in the following year they returned −1.50 percentage points relative to peers (t = −1.52), but they were already at −2.99 in the year before the change; after 230 partial changes, the following year was −0.12. Quantitative enhanced funds’ excess returns do not appear to be tied to individual managers, but the complete-replacement sample is small.
  • Benchmarks: mutual-fund quantitative hedge funds (公募量化对冲基金), with market exposure hedged out, have returned 2.4% annualized since 2015, vs 2.3% for Yu’e Bao (round 66); fully hedging with CSI 500 futures cost an average of about 9.8% a year in futures discount over 2016–2025 (round 74)—enhanced funds’ excess returns can be earned only by bearing full market risk.

7 Investor-level questions

For a Chinese individual investor who holds forever and files as required, the biggest influences on final wealth are not market timing or stock picking but three allocation choices: whether to sell (tax), which vehicle to hold through (tax and estate tax), and whether to bet on one country or diversify globally.

7.1 Tax and holding vehicles

For Chinese tax residents, the common online claim that “Irish accumulating ETFs are more tax-efficient” holds only if you never sell: the China–US tax treaty reduces US withholding on dividends to 10%, whereas Irish funds are withheld 15% at the fund level, which cannot be credited. Once US assets exceed $60,000, a non-US person who dies owes US estate tax at marginal rates of 26–40%: on $300,000, about $75,000, which over 40 years of holding is equivalent to 0.7 percentage points less a year; Ireland-domiciled ETFs do not count as US assets. Domestic S&P 500 QDII funds are tax-free for individuals, but in recent years their tracking difference has been 1.6–5 percentage points a year worse (round 55).

7.3 Stock-bond and cross-currency allocation

  • Government bonds within A-shares (rounds 69, 70, 75, 89): about 3/4 CSI 1000 + 1/4 government bonds has had a maximum drawdown of −59% vs −72% since 2006, with an annualized return only 0.19 lower; using real 1000-enhanced funds and a government bond ETF (from 2019), drawdown is −27% vs −36%, with an annualized return 2.8 lower. Rotating between stocks and bonds by moving average adds only 0.45 over a fixed ratio.
  • How often to rebalance (round 109): for 3/4 + 1/4, rebalancing once at the end of each year is enough: 2005–2026 annualized 13.1% / drawdown −53%, about the same as “check monthly and rebalance when off by more than 10 percentage points”; rebalancing every month actually loses about 1 percentage point a year; never rebalancing lets stocks drift to over 90%, losing about 10 percentage points more in a crash like 2015.
  • Which bond duration (round 115): for 3/4 CSI 1000 + 1/4 government bonds, 5-year and 10-year bonds are almost the same: annualized 14.22% vs 14.21% in 2009–2017, 1.51% vs 1.78% since 2018, and the same full-period drawdown of −53.9%. Under the rules, stay with 5-year bonds. In months when the CSI 1000 fell more than 10%, 30-year bonds gained +1.6% on average, but they can lose 5–10% in a single year.
  • Is Yu’e Bao good enough for the defensive part (round 116): 2013-06 → 2026-09, 3/4 CSI 1000 + 1/4 Yu’e Bao returned 4.48% annualized, vs 4.65% with 1/4 government bonds (after fees), with maximum drawdowns of −54.1% vs −54.0%; under the rules, “Yu’e Bao is fine.” In months when the CSI 1000 fell more than 10%, government bonds gained only +0.39% on average: losing less comes mainly from holding only 3/4 in stocks, not from bonds’ hedging.
  • Switching the defensive part to an S&P QDII (round 103): over the full 2006–2026 period, annualized return is 1.57 lower and drawdown 6.3 deeper, so under the rules government bonds are kept; but since 2015, and with real funds since 2019, the QDII did better—the conclusion depends on RMB appreciation and the big A-share bull market in 2006–2014.
  • The split between RMB and USD (round 107): in a global crisis like 2008, any mix falls by about half; since 2015, all in 1000-enhanced funds had a deepest drawdown of −65%, half and half −35%, and all in global stocks −20%, while annualized returns rose in the same order (6.7%, 9.6%, 11.9%).

7.4 How to invest new money

  • Lump-sum investing beat spreading purchases over 12 months about 70% of the time in US stocks; in A-shares it is roughly a coin toss (round 71).
  • Holding cash and “waiting for a 20% drop to buy” did better in only 43% of 20-year windows in the US since 1926 (12% after 1990), with ending wealth about 3% lower on average; Japan’s long bear market after 1990 is the exception (round 104).
  • Alipay-style “smart regular investing,” in a fair comparison with the same total amount invested, adds only 0.4% over 10 years on the CSI 1000; the high returns that platforms display come mainly from investing more principal at low points (round 105). Target-profit regular investing (taking profit at 20%) is no better than plain regular investing either (round 79).
  • The exception for small amounts of new money is the fixed minimum commission per trade: when London-listed ETFs cost about $1.9 per trade and the broker pays no interest on the first $10,000 of cash, if monthly new money is less than about $300, save up for a few months before buying (optimal interval ≈ √(24 × fee per trade ÷ (expected return × monthly amount))); across 963 20-year windows in the US since 1926, at $100 a month, buying every 2 months beat buying every month 100% of the time, with a difference of about 0.5% of ending wealth.

7.5 Should a perpetual holder use leverage?

Borrowing to hold 1.5x stocks (borrowing rate = short-term bonds + 1.5%, rebalanced monthly), only the US, the winner in hindsight, reliably earned more: in 30-year windows since 1926, the median gain is 1.6 percentage points a year, and only 4% trailed 1x (round 108). With the JST global portfolio (1871–2020), leverage added only 0.4 a year, and one third of 30-year windows trailed; pooling single countries, more than half trailed. The cost is a bigger loss in crashes (US 1929–1932: 1.5x fell 95%, 1x 84%), and the gain is very sensitive to the borrowing spread: at a spread of 2.5%, the global portfolio no longer earns more. The evidence that “young people should use leverage” has the same problem as Section 7.2: it comes mainly from the US.

7.6 Should you add a small-cap value tilt?

No (round 114). In French’s developed-market data (including the US, 1990-07 → 2026-08), small-cap value, after deducting a fee difference of about 0.25 percentage points, beat the market by 1.96 percentage points a year (t 1.63), but −0.29 since 2008; the premium was almost entirely in the 2000s (+9.6). With a 30% tilt rebalanced annually, the full period gains 0.72 a year with almost the same drawdown, but its high point relative to the pure market portfolio came in April 2010, and 16 years later it is still 6.2% below it. Developed markets ex-US did better (+2.77, t 2.26), but that is not the main test.

8 Research credibility

The biggest risk in this research is self-deception: the researcher wants their own ideas to be right. Pre-registration, split-sample testing and prior probabilities are all meant to constrain this; post hoc checks show these constraints helped, but over-optimism remains.

8.2 Multiple testing

Of the 54 “pass or fail” verdicts with prior probabilities, 14 passed. Most individual verdicts require t ≥ 2 plus the same sign in both sub-periods; if all hypotheses were false, a rough estimate is that about 1–2 would pass by chance. Credibility comes more from replication across markets and periods: the moving-average rule gave “annualized return no lower, drawdown shallower” in 9 markets (the Shanghai Composite had a shallower drawdown but slightly lower annualized return), while round 98’s pass no longer held after 2022.

8.3 Main limitations

  1. Backtests are not the future. The few edges that passed are all concentrated in a small number of years (long bear markets, A-share small-cap rallies).
  2. Short samples. Enhanced index funds are assessed mainly over 2019–2025, and funding-rate arbitrage over 2020–2026.
  3. Survivorship bias is only partly addressed. Only liquidated funds that took part in offline IPO subscriptions can be recovered; JST has no countries whose stock markets went to zero.
  4. The limits of free data. Complete fund holdings, private fund AUM and analyst consensus forecasts are unavailable, so related questions can be answered only indirectly.
  5. Topic selection itself has degrees of freedom. Pre-registration constrained each round’s verdict, but which rounds to run was the researcher’s choice.
  6. Tax treatment. Reporting and crediting of overseas income may be enforced differently in different places; conclusions are given on a “filing as required” basis.

9 Conclusion

For a Chinese individual investor with very little capital who reports overseas income as required, only one source of excess return can be reliably captured from public data: CSI 1000 enhanced index funds, at about 3–5 percentage points a year after removing IPO income, and now facing the risk of crowding by quantitative private funds. BTC cash-and-carry arbitrage passed historically, but its excess return over the past year is close to 0; the US moving-average rule reliably reduces drawdowns, but for a filer who holds forever, its after-tax return is the same as buy and hold. The value of the remaining roughly 100 rounds lies in ruling things out: limit-up chasing, chasing new coins, waiting for pullbacks, smart regular investing, valuation timing, chasing hot indexes and last year’s champion funds, long-term leverage, and adding a small-cap value tilt are all no better than the simplest approach.

The result of the research is an allocation simpler than the one it started with. For the US dollar portion, hold an Ireland-domiciled accumulating all-world stock ETF forever: global because of single-country risk, accumulating because no sale means no taxable income, and Irish because it avoids US estate tax. For the renminbi portion, spread across 3–5 low-fee CSI 1000 enhanced funds, optionally putting about a quarter in government bonds or a money market fund to reduce drawdowns (the two differ by only about 0.2 a year, round 116). Invest new money in fixed amounts as soon as you have it, without timing. The same timing rule is still useful for people who will sell or who do not pay tax—what changes the answer is tax and horizon, not the rule itself.

The next steps are forward validation and filling data gaps.

  • Forward validation: check the private 1000-enhanced crowding prediction every October; judge the A-share “most likely to fall” list once, per the pre-registration, when it reaches 12 months (around 2027-09); keep recording the moving-average rule’s monthly signals.
  • More data: redo the crowding and announcement-signal tests with private fund AUM, full fund holdings and analyst consensus forecasts from Wind or CSMAR.
  • Methodology: lower the probability of “will pass” by 5–10 percentage points at pre-registration, and keep adding each round’s result to the calibration table.
